Knowing what you don’t know

AI assistants are usually right, and sometimes completely make things up, in exactly the same confident voice. Here is why, what honest models look like, and how to protect yourself.

2When it matters

A made-up answer sounds exactly like a true one. That is the problem.

2023 · New York

Invented court cases

Lawyers filed a brief citing cases ChatGPT had made up. The judge fined them $5,000 (Mata v. Avianca).

2024 · Canada

A chatbot's refund policy

Air Canada's chatbot invented a policy. A tribunal held the airline responsible for what its chatbot said.

2025 · Australia

Fabricated references

Deloitte partially refunded the Australian government for a report with invented references and a made-up court quote.

2025 · Norway

A false accusation

ChatGPT described a Norwegian man as a child murderer. noyb filed a GDPR complaint: personal data must be accurate.

A public database tracks well over a thousand court cases where AI produced fake legal sources: AI Hallucination Cases.

3Why it happens

training

Sounding right, not being right

A model learns to continue text plausibly. Facts that appear once, or never, have no pattern to learn, but the model can still produce something with the right shape.

grading

Rewarded for guessing

Most benchmarks give zero points for “I don't know”. Like students on a multiple-choice exam, models learn that guessing pays.

calibration

Confidence that matches reality

A calibrated model is right about 9 times in 10 when it says it is 90% sure. Pretrained GPT-4 was well calibrated; assistant training reduced it.

detection

Catching a guess

Ask the same question several times. If the meanings wander, the answer is likely made up (semantic entropy, Nature 2024).

retrieval

Open book, not closed book

Searching documents first and citing them helps a lot, and lets you check.

but

Sources can be wrong too

Retrieval-based legal tools still hallucinated in 17–33% of queries; AI search engines got news sources wrong in more than 60% of tests.

4Should the model guess?

Change how wrong answers are scored and see which strategy wins. The quiz has 100 questions: the model knows 40 of them well (90% likely right), and for 60 it can only guess (25% likely right).

Penalty for a wrong answer

“I don't know” always scores 0. A right answer scores +1.

0−1−2−3

Final score

The guesseranswers all 100: about 51 right, 49 wrong
The honest modelanswers the 40 it knows: 36 right, 4 wrong, 60 × “I don't know”

The idea, and the suggestion to penalise confident errors, come from Kalai et al. 2025. On a real fact quiz (SimpleQA), o4-mini answered nearly everything and was wrong 75% of the time; gpt-5-thinking-mini declined 52% of the time and was wrong 26% of the time, with almost the same share right.
